The journey

Seven stages. Seven places the margin goes.

Each stage has its own vendor and its own logic. Nobody sees all seven — which is why the real cost of an import is almost never the one that was quoted.

Stage 01

Supplier and purchase

The negotiation sets the Incoterm, and the Incoterm sets who pays what, who insures, and where the risk transfers. Plenty of companies discover this after signing.

An Incoterm that does not suit your operation
Origin charges nobody quoted
Packing and consolidation left unoptimized
Stage 02

Classification and regulation

The tariff code decides duties, permits and standards. A convenient code today is an assessment with penalties tomorrow.

An inherited tariff code nobody revalidated
A standard or permit caught at customs, not before
Trade agreements and preferences left unused
Stage 03

International transport

Air, ocean or ground is not a price decision, it is an inventory decision. Paying for air freight because of poor planning is the most expensive saving there is.

Monthly emergencies solved with air freight
Rates never compared across forwarders
Freight left unconsolidated across suppliers
Stage 04

Documentation

A missing document does not hold up a piece of paper. It holds up a container, and a held container bills by the day.

Demurrage and storage from a late document
Incomplete files when an audit arrives
Data that does not match between invoice and entry
Stage 05

Customs and clearance

Your customs broker does their job. The question is whether anyone on your side reviews fees, charges, times and incidents with the same attention.

Ancillary charges nobody audits
Inspections triggered by an avoidable risk profile
A single broker with no point of comparison
Stage 06

Domestic transport and warehousing

The last leg is usually the worst measured, and it is where the goods are already worth everything that came before.

Handling and detention outside the agreed rate
Inventory stalled by a partial delivery
Routes that were never redesigned
Stage 07

Control and closure

If the file never closes and real cost is never compared against the estimate, the next operation repeats the same mistake under a different entry number.

A real cost per shipment nobody calculates
Incidents with no owner and no follow-up
Zero indicators to negotiate with vendors

Questions

Asked on every first call.

Are you a customs broker?

No, and the distinction matters. In Mexico customs clearance is legally reserved to a licensed customs broker. logistika coordinates, supervises and audits yours, and answers for the result of the whole operation — but does not replace their legal function or charge for it.

Are you a forwarder or a carrier?

Also no. We own no trucks, ships or warehouses, and that is an advantage: we can select and compare vendors based on your operation rather than on what we have sitting idle in a yard.

What happens to our current customs broker?

They stay, if they perform. Part of the work is evaluating them with data — times, charges, incidents — and telling you honestly whether to keep, renegotiate or compare. Changing vendors is a possible conclusion, not a goal.

How do you charge?

Start is a fixed-price project. Manage is a monthly retainer plus a fee per shipment. Partner is a monthly retainer on an annual agreement. In all three, scope and price are agreed in writing before work begins.

Do you work with foreign companies entering Mexico?

Yes, and it is one of the most natural fits: a company from Europe, Asia or the United States that will sell or manufacture here needs someone to run the Mexican side of the chain from the first shipment.
